## Context Engineering & Math Notation

For context engineering, I'd judge the math by one test: a model reading any chunk on its own should get exactly the mathematics the author meant, in the notation it reads most fluently, at the lowest token cost, with nothing it would have to look up elsewhere.

That test leads to a few properties.

1. **LaTeX, always.** Models have read far more LaTeX than any other math notation, and it is compact. MathML costs several times the tokens. Unicode inside math (`Σ ∈ 𝒞`) mixes two encodings for one symbol. Use LaTeX inside `$…$` and Unicode only in prose.
2. **Each chunk stands alone.** Author macros are expanded in the body, so a retrieved equation never depends on a preamble the model can't see.
3. **One spelling per notion, across the paper and ideally across the collection.** Then `\mathcal{C}_{++}^{n}` matches itself in retrieval, deduplication and embeddings, in every paper by the same author.
4. **Interpretation sits beside the math, never inside it.** Equations stay as the author wrote them, canonicalized. Meaning goes in a generated notation block at the top: each symbol, what it denotes in the author's words, where it's defined, and whether that is stated or inferred. This is where your D2/D3 declarations and definitions pay off. For an agent implementing a method, knowing that `\Theta` is defined in §3 and what `Diag` does matters more than any formatting choice.
5. **Show only what the paper shows.** Numbered equations keep the author's numbers via `\tag{1}`, so "Equation (1)" in the prose resolves. Stable ids for unnumbered displays live in the index, not in the text, because every inline anchor is context noise.
6. **Equation text is identity.** Sentence punctuation that trails a display (`…\rVert_{F},`) is dropped, so the same formula has the same string everywhere.
7. **Spellings that suit the host.** `\|` breaks GFM tables, so norms are spelled `\lVert…\rVert`. That is an equivalent spelling, and it survives in table cells.
8. **Theorem-like blocks are labeled units.** A definition or proposition becomes a bold label plus its statement, so it's addressable as "Proposition 4.1" without loading the paper.

### 3 New Geometries

The Cholesky decomposition is the map $Chol:\mathcal{S}_{++}^{n}\to\mathcal{L}_{+}^{n}$. For any $\Sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{++}^{n}$, $Chol(\Sigma)=L$ ensures $\Sigma=LL^{\top}$.

The Euclidean-Cholesky metric (ECM) uses the map $\Theta:\mathcal{C}_{++}^{n}\to\mathcal{L}_{1}^{n}$, where for any $C\in\mathcal{C}_{++}^{n}$

$$
\Theta(C)=Diag(Chol(C))^{-1}\cdot Chol(C)
$$

Under ECM, the distance between $C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{++}^{n}$ is

$$
d_{\text{ECM}}(C_{1},C_{2})=\lVert\Theta(C_{1})-\Theta(C_{2})\rVert_{F}\tag{1}
$$

The quotient-affine distance, by contrast, requires an optimization:

$$
d_{\mathcal{C}_{++}^{n}}^{2}(P,Q)=\underset{D\in\mathcal{D}_{++}^{n}}{\min}d_{\mathcal{S}_{++}^{n}}^{2}(P,DQD)
$$

**Proposition 4.1.** For $C_{i},C_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{++}^{n}$ and $\theta\geq 0$, the squared exponential kernel

$$
k(C_{i},C_{j})=\exp(-\theta\cdot d_{*}^{2}(C_{i},C_{j}))\tag{4}
$$

is positive-definite when $d_{*}=d_{\text{ECM}}$ or $d_{*}=d_{\text{LEC}}$.
